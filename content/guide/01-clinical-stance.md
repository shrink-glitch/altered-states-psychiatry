<h2 id="why">Why this matters</h2>

Most patients seen in Indian psychiatric practice hold religious or spiritual beliefs, and many will have consulted a temple, dargah, church, healer or guru before reaching a psychiatrist. The WPA position statement (2016) asks psychiatrists to take a spiritual history, to respect patients' beliefs, and to be aware of their own beliefs and how these might bias assessment.

Two errors are common and both cause harm:

- **Pathologising by default** - treating every unusual religious or mystical experience as psychosis. This damages trust, drives patients back to purely traditional care, and can lead to unnecessary antipsychotic exposure.
- **Romanticising** - accepting a "spiritual awakening" explanation for what is mania, psychosis, delirium or intoxication. This delays treatment and can be dangerous.

The task is not to decide whether an experience is "really" spiritual. It is to assess **safety, function and the presence of a treatable disorder**, while respecting the meaning the patient gives it.

<h2 id="history">Taking a spiritual history</h2>

A brief structured history works better than ad hoc questions. FICA (Puchalski & Romer, 2000) is one widely used format:

| | Ask about |
|---|---|
| **F** - Faith and belief | "Do you consider yourself spiritual or religious? What gives your life meaning?" |
| **I** - Importance | "How important is this to you? Has it influenced how you see this illness?" |
| **C** - Community | "Are you part of a religious or spiritual community? Is it a support?" |
| **A** - Address in care | "How would you like us to take this into account in your treatment?" |

For presentations involving practice (meditation, yoga, kriya, fasting, pilgrimage, ritual) or substances, add:

- What practice, how intensive, for how long, under whose guidance
- Changes in sleep, food and fluid intake during the practice
- Any substance use, including bhang, alcohol, cannabis, and psychedelics taken abroad or at retreats
- What the patient and family believe is happening, and what the teacher or community has said

<h2 id="language">Language that keeps the door open</h2>

Separate two questions out loud with patients and families:

1. **What does this experience mean?** This belongs to the patient and their tradition. You do not need to agree or disagree.
2. **Is the person safe, sleeping, eating and able to function?** This is the clinical question, and it is yours to answer.

Phrases that help:

- "I am not here to judge whether this is spiritual. I want to make sure your body and mind are safe while it is happening."
- "Many traditions say intense practice should be grounded and supervised. Right now your sleep has broken down, and that needs treating first."

Phrases to avoid: "This is just your illness talking", "There is no such thing as Kundalini", or, equally, "This sounds like a genuine awakening" in a patient you have not fully assessed.
