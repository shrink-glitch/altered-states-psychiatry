<h2 id="dsm">DSM-5-TR - Religious or Spiritual Problem (Z65.8)</h2>

Introduced in DSM-IV after the proposal by Lukoff, Lu and Turner (1992), this sits among "other conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention". It is **not a mental disorder**. Examples include distressing loss or questioning of faith, conversion, and distressing spiritual experiences that do not meet criteria for a disorder.

Use it when:
- the religious or spiritual issue is the focus of the consultation, **and**
- no mental disorder better accounts for the presentation, or the issue is a separate focus alongside a disorder.

It is under-used. Coding it legitimises follow-up for patients who need support but not medication.

<h2 id="icd11">ICD-11 - trance and possession trance disorders</h2>

These are dissociative disorders and are highly relevant in South Asian practice.

- **6B62 Trance disorder** - marked alteration in consciousness or loss of the usual sense of personal identity, with narrowed awareness and stereotyped behaviour.
- **6B63 Possession trance disorder** - as above, but the usual sense of identity is replaced by an external "possessing" identity (a spirit, deity or power).

The key requirement for both: the state is **involuntary**, repeated or prolonged, causes distress or impairment, and is **not accepted as part of a collective cultural or religious practice**. A possession state during a temple festival that the community expects, that is time-limited and that the person returns from without distress is not a disorder.

<h2 id="lukoff">Lukoff's criteria - mystical experience with psychotic features</h2>

Lukoff (1985) described experiences that overlap with both mystical experience and psychosis, and proposed indicators of a **good prognosis**:

1. Good pre-episode functioning
2. Acute onset of symptoms during a period of 3 months or less
3. A stressful precipitant
4. A positive exploratory attitude towards the experience

**How to use them correctly.** These criteria predict that an episode is likely to resolve without long-term illness. They do **not** tell you that the person is safe right now, or that treatment is unnecessary. A patient can meet all four and still need admission because they have not slept for five nights or are at risk.

<h2 id="spiritual-emergency">Spiritual emergency</h2>

Stanislav and Christina Grof (1989) described "spiritual emergency" as a crisis of personal transformation that can resemble psychosis, and argued it should be supported rather than suppressed.

- **Useful as a stance:** it reminds clinicians to ask what the experience means to the patient, and to avoid unnecessary coercion.
- **Not useful as a diagnosis:** there are no validated criteria, no controlled outcome data, and the concept has been used to argue against treatment of patients who were in fact psychotic or manic.

If a patient or family uses the term, engage with it respectfully, then do the same safety and differential assessment you would do for anyone else.
