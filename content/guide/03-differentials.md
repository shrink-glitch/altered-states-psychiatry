<h2 id="table">Differentials at a glance</h2>

| Differential | Points towards it | Key questions and tests |
|---|---|---|
| **Spiritual or religious problem (no disorder)** | Preserved sleep, self-care and function; experience fits the person's tradition; can reflect on it; no risk | Collateral from family; does the community recognise it as normal? |
| **Acute and transient psychotic disorder** | Abrupt onset, rapidly shifting delusions and mood, perplexity, often after a stressor or sleep loss | Onset timeline; sleep; previous episodes |
| **Mania (bipolar I)** | Decreased need for sleep without tiredness, pressured speech, grandiosity, overspending, disinhibition | Past depression, antidepressant use, family history of bipolar disorder |
| **Schizophrenia spectrum (first episode or relapse)** | Prodrome over months, negative symptoms, functional decline before the "spiritual" episode | Premorbid functioning; duration; family history |
| **Substance-induced psychosis** | Recent cannabis, bhang, stimulants, psychedelics or alcohol withdrawal | Urine drug screen; timeline of use |
| **Hallucinogen persisting perception disorder / depersonalisation** | Visual disturbances or unreality persisting after a psychedelic experience, with insight preserved | What was taken, when; insight |
| **Dissociative trance or possession trance disorder** | Episodic, identity replaced, amnesia, occurs outside culturally sanctioned settings | Context; voluntariness; distress |
| **Temporal lobe epilepsy** | Brief stereotyped episodes, déjà vu, epigastric aura, automatisms, ecstatic or religious auras | EEG; neurology review |
| **Delirium** | Fluctuating consciousness, disorientation, after fasting or dehydration | Bedside cognition; electrolytes; glucose |

<h2 id="mania">Mania with religious content</h2>

Religious and spiritual themes are common in mania - a mission from God, special powers, an awakening. The content does not change the diagnosis. Look for:

- **Decreased need for sleep without fatigue** - the single most useful discriminating question. People in a spiritual crisis who are not sleeping usually feel exhausted.
- Pressured speech, flight of ideas, increased goal-directed activity
- Overspending or giving money away, often framed as renunciation or donation
- A past history of depression, particularly postpartum, and recent antidepressant exposure

Always get collateral history. Patients in mania rarely describe these features themselves.

<h2 id="atpd">Acute and transient psychotic disorder</h2>

The most common outcome in published case series of meditation-associated psychosis (Kuijpers 2007; Sharma 2022). Features:

- Onset within 2 weeks, often within days
- Rapidly changing symptoms - ecstatic one hour, terrified the next
- Precipitants: intensive practice, sleep deprivation, fasting, social isolation, substance use
- Usually resolves fully within weeks to 3 months

ATPD is **diagnostically unstable**: a meaningful proportion of patients later receive a diagnosis of schizophrenia or bipolar disorder. Plan follow-up for at least a year.

<h2 id="organic">Organic causes not to miss</h2>

- **Focal epilepsy** - temporal lobe epilepsy is classically linked with religious experiences, and ecstatic auras are now also linked to the anterior insula. Consider it with brief, stereotyped episodes, involuntary movements, or altered awareness. See [ecstatic and religious seizures](#epilepsy).
- **Delirium** - after prolonged fasting, dehydration, or in older pilgrims. Check orientation, attention, electrolytes and glucose.
- **Thyroid disease** - hyperthyroidism can mimic mania.
- **Intoxication and withdrawal** - including alcohol withdrawal after a dry retreat period, and cannabis or bhang during festivals.

Minimum work-up for a first presentation: bedside cognitive screen, complete blood count, electrolytes, glucose, thyroid function, urine drug screen. Add EEG and neuroimaging when the history suggests it.

<h2 id="cannabis">Cannabis and bhang</h2>

- **Edible forms** (bhang thandai, bhang lassi, cannabis sweets) start working after 30 minutes to 2 hours and last many hours. People often take more because "nothing is happening", then become severely intoxicated.
- **Intoxication** can include panic, paranoia and brief psychotic symptoms. Most settles with time, reassurance and, if needed, a benzodiazepine.
- **Substance-induced psychotic disorder**: psychotic symptoms that persist beyond the expected course of intoxication. If symptoms continue more than about a month after stopping, reconsider a primary psychotic disorder.
- **Long-term risk**: in a Danish registry study of 6,788 people with substance-induced psychosis, 47% of those with cannabis-induced psychosis later received a diagnosis of schizophrenia or bipolar disorder (Starzer 2018). Higher levels of use carry higher risk (Marconi 2016).
- **Legal context in India**: bhang, made from cannabis leaves, is excluded from the NDPS Act's definition of cannabis and is regulated by states; ganja and charas are controlled. This matters for how patients talk about use, not for clinical risk.

<h2 id="hallucinogens">Psychedelics and HPPD</h2>

Patients increasingly report psychedelic use at retreats abroad (ayahuasca in South America, psilocybin in the Netherlands) or at festivals.

- **Take a non-judgemental history**: substance, number of sessions, setting, other substances, and how prescribed medication was handled. Ayahuasca contains MAO-inhibiting alkaloids; combined with serotonergic antidepressants it can cause serotonin toxicity.
- **Hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD)**: re-experiencing of perceptual changes after use - trails, halos, visual snow, intensified colours - with **preserved insight**, causing distress. Derealisation and anxiety are commonly associated (Halpern 2018).
- **Not psychosis**: insight into the perceptual nature of the symptoms is the key distinction.
- **Differentials**: visual snow syndrome, migraine aura, eye disease, depersonalisation-derealisation disorder, and a new psychotic or mood disorder unmasked by the drug.
- **Management**: explanation and reassurance, treatment of anxiety, avoiding cannabis and further hallucinogens. Evidence for specific medication is limited to case reports and small series.

<h2 id="delirium">Delirium after fasting or pilgrimage</h2>

Older pilgrims and people on long religious fasts are at risk of delirium, which often comes with vivid, religiously coloured visual hallucinations at night.

- **Core feature**: inattention with fluctuating alertness, developing over hours to days (Inouye 2014). Test attention directly, for example months of the year backwards.
- **Common precipitants**: dehydration, very low food and salt intake with high water intake, heat, infection, and medication - particularly thiazide diuretics, which cause **hyponatraemia**.
- **Management**: treat the cause; correct chronic hyponatraemia slowly under medical supervision because rapid correction risks osmotic demyelination (Spasovski 2014); use non-drug measures (orientation, family presence, glasses and hearing aids, sleep) first; antipsychotics only for severe distress or risk.
- **Afterwards**: reassess cognition, and plan any future fasting with the patient, family and physician.

<h2 id="epilepsy">Ecstatic and religious seizures</h2>

- **Typical history**: brief (seconds to a couple of minutes), stereotyped episodes; a warning such as a rising epigastric sensation or déjà vu; sometimes impaired awareness with automatisms (lip-smacking, fumbling); confusion afterwards.
- **Ecstatic auras**: intense well-being, clarity and heightened self-awareness, sometimes experienced as the presence of God. Picard and Craig (2009) linked these to the anterior insula rather than only the temporal lobe.
- **Risk factors**: febrile seizures in childhood, head injury, family history.
- **Work-up**: EEG (with a sleep-deprived recording if the first is normal) and epilepsy-protocol MRI, with neurology.
- **Safety**: ask about driving, swimming and work at heights.
- **Meaning**: patients may value the experiences and fear losing them with treatment. Discuss this openly.
