<h2 id="red-flags">Red flags</h2>

Any of these means the presentation needs active clinical management, regardless of how the patient or community understands it:

- Suicidal ideation, self-harm, or risk to others, including risk from commands attributed to a deity or spirit
- Sleep of fewer than about 3 hours a night for several nights
- Not eating or drinking adequately
- Confusion, disorientation or fluctuating consciousness
- Function not recovering after a few weeks
- Past history of psychosis or bipolar disorder, or a strong family history
- Postpartum onset
- Recent use of cannabis, stimulants, or psychedelics combined with lithium or other serotonergic drugs

<h2 id="principles">Management principles</h2>

1. **Safety first.** Assess risk and decide on the setting of care.
2. **Restore sleep.** This is often the single most effective intervention, and an easy one for patients and families to accept.
3. **Pause intensive practice.** Frame this as grounding, which most contemplative traditions themselves recommend during difficulty.
4. **Treat any disorder present** using standard guidelines. Short courses of antipsychotics for ATPD; mood stabilisers for mania.
5. **Work with the family and, with consent, the teacher or community.** Many meditation and yoga organisations have their own guidance for practitioners in difficulty and can be allies.
6. **Code what you see.** If no disorder is present, use the DSM-5-TR Z65.8 code and offer follow-up anyway.

<h2 id="return">Returning to practice</h2>

Most patients will want to return to their practice. Forbidding it rarely works and damages the relationship. Instead:

- Advise against intensive or residential retreats until well for several months
- Prefer shorter sessions, under a teacher who knows the history
- Agree early warning signs with the patient and family - usually sleep loss first
- Many residential meditation courses ask about psychiatric history on their application form; encourage honest disclosure
- Revisit the plan at each follow-up
